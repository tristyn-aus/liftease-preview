Home Elevator vs Home Lifts: What's the Difference?

25 June 2026

If you’ve been researching ways to add vertical access to your home, you’ve probably come across both terms — home elevator and home lift — and wondered whether they mean the same thing. You’re not alone. It’s one of the most common questions we get at Lift Ease, and the short answer is: they’re often used interchangeably, but there are some real differences worth understanding before you invest.

In this guide we’ll break down exactly what separates a home elevator from a home lift, what each is best suited for, and how to decide which option is right for your Melbourne home.

Are “Home Elevator” and “Home Lift” the Same Thing?

In everyday conversation, yes — most people use them interchangeably. A home elevator is a residential elevator installed in a private dwelling. A home lift does exactly the same job. The terminology simply varies by region, brand, and building industry convention.

In Australia, both terms are widely used. “Home lift Melbourne” and “home elevator Melbourne” appear in hundreds of searches every month by homeowners looking for the exact same product. So whether you’ve been googling home lifts or residential elevators, you’re looking at the same category of product.

That said, within the industry there are some distinctions worth knowing about — particularly around lift type, travel height, and the regulatory standards that apply.

The Key Differences: What Actually Varies

1. Travel Height and Number of Levels

A residential lift or home lift commonly refers to a shorter-travel unit designed to move between two levels — typically ground floor to first floor. These are sometimes called platform lifts or through floor lifts, and they’re a popular, cost-effective solution for homes that need accessibility without a full elevator shaft.

A home elevator or residential elevator generally refers to a fully enclosed cab that travels in a dedicated shaft or hoistway, can service multiple levels (two, three, or more storeys), and operates much like a commercial elevator — just scaled for private residential use.

If your home has three or more levels, or you want a fully enclosed, door-operated lift, a residential elevator is typically the right product. If you need a practical, space-efficient solution for a single level change, a home lift or through floor lift Melbourne may be all you need.

2. Enclosure and Cab Design

Home elevators are fully enclosed — four walls, a ceiling, doors on one or both sides, and an interior cab that passengers stand inside. They’re designed to be a permanent, integrated feature of the home’s architecture.

Some home lifts — particularly platform lifts and stairway-adjacent units — are open or semi-enclosed. They move a platform rather than a cab. These are generally used in accessibility-focused applications rather than lifestyle upgrades.

At Lift Ease, our residential elevator range features fully enclosed, architecturally designed cabs built to complement your home’s interior — not detract from it.

3. Drive Systems: How They Move

This is where things get interesting, and where the terminology can overlap significantly.

Hydraulic elevators use a fluid-driven piston system. They’re smooth, reliable, and well-suited to homes with a dedicated machine room or pit space.

Traction elevators use a cable and counterweight system. They’re efficient over longer travel distances and are common in multi-storey residential builds.

Vacuum elevators (also called pneumatic elevators) operate via air pressure differential — no cables, no hydraulics, just a transparent tube and air. Vacuum elevator Melbourne searches have been growing steadily as homeowners look for designs that double as a visual feature.

Screw-driven or rack-and-pinion lifts are compact, require no machine room, and suit smaller home elevator installation Melbourne projects where space is at a premium.

4. Shaft Requirements and Installation

A traditional residential elevator requires a dedicated shaft — either built into a new home during construction or retrofitted into an existing structure. Home elevator installation Melbourne in an existing property typically involves a structural assessment, some construction work, and a longer lead time.

A small home elevator or through floor lift can often be installed with far less structural disruption. Some models require only a modest floor opening and a compact footprint, making them ideal for retrofits in established Melbourne homes.

5. Capacity and Intended Use

Domestic lift and residential elevator products span a wide range of capacities. Entry-level home lifts may be designed to carry one or two people and a light load. Full residential elevators are typically rated for 3–5 passengers and can handle mobility equipment, shopping, or moving items between floors.

If you’re planning ahead for ageing in place — or need to accommodate a wheelchair or mobility device — a full residential elevator rated for the appropriate load is the better long-term investment.

Which One Is Right for Your Home?

The right choice depends on four things: your home’s structure, how many levels you need to service, how you’ll use it, and your budget.

A through floor lift or small home elevator suits: a two-storey home, a practical accessibility need, a tighter budget, or a retrofit where major construction isn’t feasible.

A full residential elevator suits: a three-or-more storey home, a new build where the shaft can be planned in from the start, a lifestyle upgrade rather than just a functional one, or a homeowner who wants an integrated, architectural feature that adds genuine value to the property.

Both add value. Both improve liveability. The question is simply which one fits your home and your vision.

Home Elevator Cost Australia: What to Expect

One of the most common searches we see alongside these terms is home elevator cost Australia — and it’s a fair question. The honest answer is that cost varies significantly depending on the type of lift, the number of levels, the shaft requirements, the cab finish, and the complexity of the installation.

What we can say is that a residential elevator is a meaningful investment — and one that typically adds more to your property’s value than it costs to install, particularly in the Melbourne market where multi-storey homes are increasingly common.

For an accurate home lift cost Australia figure for your specific home, the best starting point is always a site consultation. Every property is different, and a proper quote requires understanding your structure, your access requirements, and what you want the lift to do.

Frequently Asked Questions

What Is the Difference Between a Home Lift and a Home Elevator?

The terms are used interchangeably in Australia. Both refer to a lift installed in a private residence. Technically, a home elevator typically implies a fully enclosed, multi-storey unit, while a home lift can also refer to smaller platform or through floor lift products.

Do I Need Council Approval for a Home Elevator in Melbourne?

In most cases, yes — a residential elevator is a structural addition that requires a building permit. Your installer will typically manage this process as part of the project.

How Long Does Home Elevator Installation in Melbourne Take?

Installation time varies by model. Levare lifts take about 2 days, home lifts such as the Vector Pro, Zenith Ultra and Atlus take 1–2 weeks, the RB150 platform lift within a week, and commercial lifts (Zenith DDA, E1) 2–4 weeks. Any building work needed for a new shaft is carried out before installation.

What Is a Vacuum Elevator?

A vacuum elevator (or pneumatic elevator) operates using air pressure to move the cab up and down inside a transparent cylinder. They require no pit, no machine room, and no cables, making them a popular choice for retrofits and design-conscious homeowners.

Can a Home Lift Be Installed in an Existing House?

Yes. Many home lift and small home elevator products are specifically designed for retrofit installation in existing Melbourne homes. The key factors are ceiling height, available footprint, and floor structure.
