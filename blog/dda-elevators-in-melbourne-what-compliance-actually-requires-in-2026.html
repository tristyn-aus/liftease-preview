DDA Elevators in Melbourne: What Compliance Actually Requires in 2026

25 August 2026

What Does DDA Mean?

If you have been told your Melbourne project needs a “DDA lift”, you have probably also discovered that no Australian standard or code actually uses that phrase. It is industry shorthand, and it covers a fair bit of ground. Knowing what sits behind it is the difference between a lift that passes inspection and one that gets flagged by your building surveyor after the shaft is already poured.

Here is what the term means in practice, which rules apply in Victoria right now, and where projects most often come unstuck.

Where the obligation comes from

The Disability Discrimination Act 1992 (DDA) is Commonwealth law. It makes it unlawful to discriminate against a person with a disability in the provision of access to premises used by the public. It does not contain lift dimensions or button heights. It creates a duty, and complaints under it are made to the Australian Human Rights Commission.

The technical detail lives further down the chain. The Disability (Access to Premises — Buildings) Standards 2010 give the DDA a buildable form, and their access requirements are mirrored in the National Construction Code. In Victoria the Building Act 1993 and Building Regulations 2018 give the NCC legal effect, with the Building and Plumbing Commission overseeing compliance. The lift-specific detail comes from AS 1735.12, which sets out facilities for people with disabilities, along with the relevant parts of the AS 1735 series for the lift type you are installing.

So when someone says DDA elevator, what they usually mean is a passenger lift that satisfies NCC Part E3 and AS 1735.12 in a building that is required to be accessible.

Victoria is on NCC 2025

This matters for anyone pricing work at the moment. Victoria adopted NCC 2025 on 1 May 2026 with no state-wide transition period, while New South Wales and Queensland deferred to 1 May 2027. Section 10 of the Building Act allows a project to stay on NCC 2022 where the applicant can demonstrate to their building surveyor that design work had substantially commenced before the changeover, but the surveyor decides that on a project-by-project basis.

If you work across state borders, do not assume a specification that cleared a certifier in Sydney will clear one in Melbourne. Confirm the applicable edition before you lock in a lift package.

When a building needs an accessible lift

The trigger is not the number of storeys on its own. Under the NCC, an accessible lift is required where vertical movement is needed between levels that must be accessible. In a Class 2 apartment building that generally means the path from the entry through to common areas and, depending on the design, to the units themselves. In Class 5 to 9 buildings it applies to the parts of the building people can get to.

Your building surveyor or access consultant makes that call against the specific building classification and layout. It is worth having that conversation before the architect settles on core dimensions, because a shaft that is 100mm too narrow is an expensive thing to discover late.

What makes a lift compliant

The NCC sets out car sizes, door widths, and features by lift type. For a passenger lift the headline numbers are:

  • Car floor of at least 1400mm wide by 1600mm deep for any lift travelling more than 12 metres
  • Car floor of at least 1100mm wide by 1400mm deep for lifts travelling 12 metres or less
  • A clear door opening of at least 900mm
  • A handrail at least 600mm long adjacent to the car operating panel, in accordance with AS 1735.12

Beyond dimensions, AS 1735.12 covers the parts users actually interact with. Control buttons at reachable heights with tactile and braille markings. Audible floor announcements and visual indicators. Emergency communication that works for someone who cannot hear or cannot speak. Door protection that reopens the doors without requiring contact. Adequate lighting and luminance contrast so the car edge and controls are visible to someone with low vision.

The landing outside the lift is part of the compliance picture too. A car that meets every dimension in the code is no use if the approach does not give a wheelchair user room to turn and wait clear of the doors.

Not every lift type is allowed everywhere

This is where projects trip up. The NCC permits a range of lift types, including stairway platform lifts, low-rise platform lifts, and low-rise low-speed constant pressure lifts. Each comes with limits.

Stairway platform lifts cannot be used where another type of passenger lift could be installed. Several of the smaller types are restricted to connecting two storeys, are capped by occupant numbers, and are excluded from high-traffic public areas. A constant pressure lift at the entry to a theatre is not acceptable, though the same lift providing access to the stage may be.

Access consultants have also raised real concerns about AS 1735.15 and vertical platform lifts, particularly around sill thresholds and landing clearances that depart from AS 1428.1 expectations. Specifying a platform lift because it is cheaper and fits the available space can leave a building exposed to a DDA complaint even where a surveyor has signed it off. Get advice on the type before you get quotes.

Retrofitting an existing Melbourne building

Much of the accessible lift work in Melbourne is retrofit. Warehouse conversions in Collingwood, older walk-up apartment blocks in St Kilda, heritage-overlay shopfronts in the CBD with a first-floor tenancy that has never had access.

Two things are worth understanding. First, the Premises Standards apply to new buildings and to new work, including the affected part of an existing building, so a renovation can pull an accessibility obligation with it. Second, the DDA duty on a building owner does not switch off simply because the building predates the legislation. The Act allows for unjustifiable hardship, but that is a defence assessed on the facts, not a box you tick.

Practically, retrofit usually comes down to whether you can find the footprint and the pit depth. Machine-room-less and reduced-pit configurations open up options that did not exist a decade ago, and a shaft that looked impossible in 2015 is often workable now.

Private homes are a different question

The DDA does not regulate a private residence. If you are putting a lift in your own home in Balwyn or Brighton, no one is auditing it against AS 1735.12.

That said, plenty of homeowners ask for the same features anyway, and for good reason. If the lift is going in so a parent can stay in the house, or so the house still works after a knee replacement, then car size, door width, control height, and a handrail are exactly the things that determine whether it does the job in ten years. Building to the accessible spec in a home is a design choice rather than a legal one, and it is usually a sensible choice.
