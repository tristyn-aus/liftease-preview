Best Home Lift for a Small Townhouse (Plus Retrofit Options for Melbourne Homes)

22 July 2026

If you live in a townhouse, you already know the trade-off: fantastic location, low maintenance, and barely enough floor space to swing a cat. So when stairs start becoming a problem — whether from an ageing hip, a growing family, or simply planning ahead — the idea of “just install a lift” can feel impossible. Where would it even go?

The good news is that home lifts have changed a lot in the last decade. You no longer need a full lift shaft, a machine room, or a chunk of your living room sacrificed to make multi-level living work. For Melbourne’s tightly packed terraces, narrow inner-city townhouses, and modern medium-density builds, there are now compact, retrofit-friendly options built specifically for homes where space is the number one constraint.

Why Townhouses Need a Different Approach to Home Lifts

Standard residential lifts are often designed with new-build, larger-footprint homes in mind. Townhouses present a different set of challenges:

  • Narrow floor plates – many townhouses are built on long, narrow blocks, leaving little room for anything beyond stairs and a hallway.
  • Limited pit and headroom – traditional lifts often need a recessed pit below the floor and significant headroom above the top landing, which older or split-level townhouses simply don’t have.
  • Shared or party walls – any structural work needs to be carefully planned so it doesn’t impact adjoining properties.
  • Heritage overlays and planning controls – common in inner Melbourne suburbs, these can restrict changes to a building’s external profile or footprint.

This is exactly why “small footprint” and “low pit/no pit” matter more than raw lift speed or cabin size when you’re comparing options.

What to Look for in a Small-Footprint Home Lift

  1. Minimal or zero pit depth – some modern hydraulic and screw-drive lifts need only a shallow recess (or none at all), avoiding costly excavation.
  2. Self-supporting structure – lifts with a self-supporting shaft don’t rely on load-bearing walls, which means they can often be installed without major structural changes to the home.
  3. Compact cabin footprint – look for models designed around a single-person or small-couple cabin size rather than commercial-scale dimensions.
  4. Quiet, low-vibration operation – important in townhouses where the lift may sit close to bedrooms or living areas due to limited placement options.
  5. Through-floor or platform lift designs – for very tight sites, a through-floor lift can be one of the least invasive ways to connect two levels.

Retrofit Home Lifts in Melbourne: What’s Actually Involved

Retrofitting a lift into an existing townhouse is a different project to installing one during a new build, but it’s far more achievable than most homeowners expect. A well-planned retrofit typically involves:

  • A site assessment to confirm structural capacity, available space (often a former cupboard, stairwell void, or corner of a room), and access for installation.
  • Council and planning considerations, particularly if your townhouse falls within a heritage overlay or has strata/owners corporation requirements.
  • Electrical and structural coordination, usually completed by trades working alongside your lift installer.
  • A compact, purpose-built lift model chosen to suit the space rather than forcing a standard footprint into a space that wasn’t designed for it.

Because so many Melbourne townhouses share similar constraints — narrow lots, established streetscapes, and limited side or rear access — an installer with genuine local retrofit experience makes a real difference to how smoothly the project runs.

Getting the Right Fit for Your Home

Every townhouse is different, and the “best” home lift really comes down to your specific floor plan, budget, and how the lift will be used day to day. As a starting point, it’s worth having a straightforward conversation about:

  • How much floor space you can realistically dedicate (even 700–900mm can be enough for some models)
  • Whether you’re working with an existing void, stairwell, or open corner
  • Any heritage or strata approvals that may apply
  • Your timeline and whether the home is occupied during installation
